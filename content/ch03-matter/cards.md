# Deck 1: States of Matter

## What matter is

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Matter | Anything that takes up space (has volume) and has mass. | No mass and no space means not matter. |
| Solid | Rigid state with a fixed volume and a fixed shape. Its particles are locked in rigid positions, close together (they still vibrate). Examples: ice cube, diamond, iron bar. | Solid = stuck in place. |
| Liquid | State with a definite volume that takes the shape of its container. Its particles are close together but can move around. Examples: gasoline, water, alcohol, blood. | Liquid = fills the bottom of the bowl. |
| Gas | State with no fixed volume and no fixed shape: it takes both the shape and the volume of its container. Its particles are far apart and move randomly. Examples: air, helium, oxygen. | Gas = goes everywhere. |

## Water at the particle level

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Ice (solid water) | Water molecules locked into rigid positions, close together. | Ice = a fixed grid. |
| Liquid water | Water molecules still close together but able to move around to some extent. | Liquid = close but sliding. |
| Steam (gaseous water) | Water molecules far apart, moving randomly and hitting each other and the container walls. | Steam = spread out and zooming. |
| Intact molecules | In ice, liquid water and steam the H₂O molecules are still intact. Only the motion of the molecules and the distances between them change. | Same molecule, different spacing. |

## Particle motion in each state

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Solid particles | Particles vibrate in fixed positions, closely packed and orderly. | Solid = vibrate in place. |
| Liquid particles | Particles are close together but slide past each other. | Liquid = close but sliding. |
| Gas particles | Particles are far apart, moving fast and randomly. | Gas = far apart and fast. |
| Energy and state | Energy increases from solid to liquid to gas. More energy means more motion and less order. | More energy = more motion = less order. |

## Changes of state (part 1 of 2)

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | Solid to liquid. A physical change. The particles speed up and become less organized. | Melting = solid to liquid. |
| Freezing | Liquid to solid. A physical change. The particles slow down and become more organized. | Freezing = liquid to solid. |
| Evaporation or boiling | Liquid to gas. A physical change. The particles speed up and become less organized. | Boiling = liquid to gas. |

## Changes of state (part 2 of 2)

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Condensation | Gas to liquid. A physical change. The particles slow down and become more organized. | Condensation = gas to liquid. |
| Sublimation | Solid to gas, skipping the liquid. A physical change. The particles speed up and become less organized. | Sublimation = solid to gas. |
| Deposition | Gas to solid, skipping the liquid. A physical change. The particles slow down and become more organized. | Deposition = gas to solid. |
| Speed up or slow down | Particles that speed up move to a state with less organization (melting, boiling, evaporation, sublimation). Particles that slow down move to a state with more organization (freezing, condensation, deposition). All six are physical changes. | Speed up = less order. Slow down = more order. |

## Water at 0 °C and absolute zero

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Water at 0 °C | At 0 °C water is at its melting and freezing point, so ice and liquid water can sit together in equilibrium. Some water molecules also escape into the air as water vapor (evaporation from the liquid and sublimation from the ice), so all three states are present. | Ice + water + a little vapor. |
| Absolute zero (0 K) | The lowest possible temperature, 0 K = -273.15 °C. Particles have their minimum possible motion, so nothing can be colder. The Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, so it has no negative numbers. | Zero Kelvin = the bottom. |

# Deck 2: Properties and Changes

## Types of properties

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Physical property | A characteristic that can be observed or measured without changing the substance into a different substance. | Look, do not change. |
| Chemical property | The ability of a substance to change into a different (new) substance. | Chemical = can become something new. |
| Intensive property | A physical property that does NOT depend on the amount of substance present. Examples: boiling point, color, temperature, luster, hardness, density, ductile (drawn into thin wires) and malleable (hammered into thin sheets). | Intensive = unchanged when you cut it in half. |
| Extensive property | A physical property that DOES depend on the amount of substance present. Examples: mass, volume, size, weight, length. | Extensive = more stuff, bigger value. |

## Examples of properties

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Physical properties you observe | Odor, color and state (solid, liquid or gas). | Use your senses. |
| Physical properties you measure | Volume, density, melting point, boiling point and hardness. | Use an instrument. |
| Ethyl alcohol boiling at 78°C is an example of what change? | A physical change: boiling is a change of state, and no new substance forms. The boiling point itself is a physical property. | Boiling point is always physical. |
| Sugar fermenting to form ethyl alcohol is an example of what change? | A chemical change: a new substance (alcohol) forms. | New substance = chemical. |

## Chemical property examples

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Flammability | The ability to burn. A chemical property, because burning makes new substances. | Flammable = can burn. |
| Rusting of steel | Steel reacting with oxygen to form rust. A chemical property. | Rust = new substance. |
| Digestion of food | Food is broken down into new substances in your body. A chemical property. | Digest = new substances. |
| Reacting with acid | Whether a substance reacts with an acid, such as vinegar on chalk. A chemical property. | Acid reaction = chemical. |
| Other chemical properties | The review sheet also lists reacting or not reacting with oxygen or water, being corrosive, being toxic, and pH. | Reacts or not, corrosive, toxic, pH. |

## Physical and chemical changes

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Physical change | A change in one or more physical properties of a substance but NOT in its chemical composition. Chemical bonds are not broken. Example: boiling or freezing water. | Same stuff, new look. |
| Chemical change | A given substance becomes a new substance or substances with different properties and different composition. The atoms are reorganized. | New stuff. |
| Reaction | Another name for a chemical change. Example: in a Bunsen burner methane reacts with oxygen to form carbon dioxide and water. | Reaction = chemical change. |

## Signs of a chemical change

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Precipitate | A solid that forms when two pure substances react. A sign that a new substance was made. | New solid = chemical. |
| Gas given off | Bubbles form because a new gas is made. A sign of a chemical change. Odor is a physical property, so a smell alone is not a sign. | Bubbles = new gas. |
| Color change | A new color can show that a new substance was made. | New color = maybe new substance. |
| Temperature change | The mixture gets hotter or colder on its own as the reaction happens. | Heats or cools by itself. |
| Light given off | A flame or glow shows that a reaction is giving off energy as light. | Glow = reaction. |

## Key cases

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Melting | Solid to liquid. A physical change of state. | Melting is physical. |
| Boiling | Liquid to gas. A physical change of state. | Boiling is physical. |
| Electrolysis of water (decomposition) | An electric current splits water (H₂O) into hydrogen (H₂) and oxygen (O₂) gases. A chemical change: two H₂O make two H₂ and one O₂. | Electric current breaks bonds. |
| Combustion (burning) | A chemical change in which a substance reacts with oxygen, giving off heat and light and forming new substances. Burning wood leaves ash and gases. | Fire = new substances. |
| Rusting | Iron combines with oxygen to form rust (iron oxide). A chemical change. | Iron + oxygen = rust. |

## Types of reactions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Synthesis | Two or more substances combine to form something NEW. | Synthesis = build up. |
| Decomposition | A substance breaks down to make two or more substances. Electrolysis of water is an example. | Decompose = break apart. |
| Single displacement | An element is "pushed out" of a compound by another element. | Single = one element swaps. |
| Double displacement | The positive elements switch places in two compounds. | Double = two partners swap. |

# Deck 3: Elements, Compounds and Mixtures

## Pure substances and mixtures

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Element | A substance that cannot be broken down into other substances by chemical methods. Examples: Fe, Al, O₂, H₂, Au. | Element = one type of atom. |
| Compound | A substance made of a given combination of elements (two or more types of atoms) that can be broken down into those elements by chemical methods. It always has the same composition. Examples: H₂O, CO₂, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. | Compound = a fixed recipe. |
| Pure substance | Matter that always has the same composition. It is either an element or a compound. | Pure means element or compound. |
| Mixture | Matter with variable composition, made of two or more pure substances that can be separated by physical means. Examples: wood, wine, coffee. | Mixture = no fixed recipe. |

## Types of mixtures

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Homogeneous mixture | A mixture that is the same throughout, with visibly indistinguishable parts. Examples: air, brass, table salt stirred into water, gasoline. | Homo = same. |
| Heterogeneous mixture | A mixture with visibly distinguishable parts and regions that have different properties. Examples: oil-and-vinegar dressing, sand stirred into water, a jar of jelly beans, soil. | Hetero = different. |
| Solution | Another name for a homogeneous mixture. | Solution = uniform mixture. |

## Reading formulas

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Subscript | A small number in a formula showing how many atoms of that type are in one molecule. H₂O has 2 H and 1 O. C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ has 45 atoms. | Subscript counts atoms. |
| The & symbol | In a formula code, & separates different substances that are just sitting together, so it signals a MIXTURE (for example NaCl & H₂O). | & means mixture. |
| Element formula clue | One capital letter (one type of atom), such as Br₂, Al, P₄, O₃. A subscript alone does not make something a compound. | One capital letter = element. |
| Compound formula clue | Two or more capital letters (different atoms) bonded in one formula with no &, such as CO₂, NaHCO₃, H₂SO₄. | Two or more capitals, no & = compound. |
| Mixture formula clue | A code with & between substances, such as C₆H₁₂O₆ & H₂O or Cu & Zn. | Look for & first. |

## Particles versus bulk samples

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Atom | A single particle of an element. Atoms can be bonded together into molecules. | Atom = one particle. |
| Molecule | Two or more atoms held together by chemical bonds. A molecule can contain one type of atom (Br₂) or several (H₂O). | Molecule = bonded atoms. |
| Particle | Any individual atom or molecule in a drawing. A single atom can be a particle and so can a molecule. | Count each separate piece. |
| Bulk terms versus particle terms | Element, compound and mixture describe bulk samples. Atom and molecule describe particles. Atom does NOT equal element and molecule does NOT equal compound (Br₂ is a molecule but an element). | Bulk = E/C/M. Particles = atom/molecule. |

## Solutions and suspensions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Solvent | The part of a solution that does the dissolving (example: water). A solution is a mixture of two or more substances. | Solvent dissolves. |
| Solute | The part of a solution that is dissolved (example: sugar). | Solute is dissolved. |
| Suspension | A heterogeneous mixture that settles upon standing. Examples: mud, juice with pulp, oil and water. | Suspension = settles. |

## Mixture examples from the review sheet

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Homogeneous examples | Salt water, lemonade, tea, coffee, 18K gold, air, steel, rain water. | Same throughout. |
| Heterogeneous examples | Coke float, sand in water, milk, chocolate chip cookies, pizza, salad. | You can see phases. |

# Deck 4: Separation of Mixtures

## Techniques

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Physical means | Methods that separate a mixture without changing the identity of its parts. No bonds are broken. | Physical = separate, do not change. |
| Distillation | Separates substances using differences in boiling point: boil, then condense the vapor. Salt water gives pure water, and the salt stays in the flask. | Distill = boiling point. |
| Filtration | Separates a liquid from an undissolved solid using filter paper (state of matter). Dissolved salt or sugar passes through the filter. | Filter = solid versus liquid. |
| Chromatography | Separates substances by how strongly they adhere to a surface: a mobile phase carries the parts of the mixture across a fixed phase, and each part travels a different distance. | Chromatography = sticks to a surface. |
| Evaporation | Separates substances using volatility: the volatile component leaves as a vapor and the other stays behind. | Evaporate = volatility. |

## Mixtures versus compounds

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Separating mixtures | Mixtures can be separated by physical methods into pure substances (elements and/or compounds). | Mixture = physical. |
| Separating compounds | Compounds need chemical methods (breaking bonds), such as electrolysis or decomposition, to be separated into elements. | Compound = chemical. |
| Distillation of salt water | A physical method: no chemical change occurs. A saltwater solution (a homogeneous mixture) becomes salt plus pure water. | Same salt, same water. |

## Magnets and chromatography

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Magnetism | A separation that works when some particles are magnetic and stick to a magnet (for example iron filings in sand). It is a physical method. | Magnet picks out the magnetic part. |
| Mobile phase | In chromatography the mobile phase (such as water) moves the parts of the mixture across the paper. Example: separating ink into its colors with water and paper. | Mobile moves. |
| Fixed phase | In chromatography the fixed phase (such as paper) does not move. The parts of the mixture stick to it by different amounts. | Fixed stays. |
| Picking pieces out | The simplest physical means: you can pick the pieces out by hand, like separating the parts of a salad. | Salad = pick it apart. |

# Deck 5: Lab Stations and Colloids

## Lab stations 1 to 3

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Station 1: paper | Tearing paper is a physical change. Igniting the paper is a chemical change (flame, heat, ash and gases). | Tear = physical, burn = chemical. |
| Station 2: tea lights | Melting a tea light on a hot plate is a physical change. Burning one is a chemical change: the wax reacts with oxygen and gives off heat and light. | Melt = physical, burn = chemical. |
| Station 3: steel wool | Burning steel wool is a chemical change: iron combines with oxygen to form iron(III) oxide, so the mass INCREASES. | Gains oxygen, gains mass. |

## Lab stations 4 to 6

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Station 4: vinegar and chalk (TUMS) | Calcium carbonate reacts with vinegar, forming bubbles of gas (a chemical change). Filtering the leftover solid is a physical separation. | Bubbles mean a new gas. |
| Station 5: zinc in hydrochloric acid | A post-1982 penny has a zinc core. Once it is cut, the zinc reacts with hydrochloric acid, bubbles form and the penny loses mass (a chemical change). | Zinc reacts, mass lost. |
| Station 6: nail and peroxide | Hydrogen peroxide reacts with the iron nail (a chemical change). It is a dangerous material, so the safety rules apply. | Peroxide plus iron: react, be careful. |

## Colloids (part 1 of 2)

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Colloid | A heterogeneous mixture that may appear homogeneous to the naked eye. | Looks smooth but is heterogeneous. |
| Colloid particle size | Colloid particles are about 1 nm to 1000 nm across. | Colloid = 1 to 1000 nm. |
| Colloid stability | Colloids are stable: the particles do not settle on standing. They cannot be separated by simple filtration. | Stable = never settles. |
| Tyndall effect | Colloidal particles scatter a light beam so the beam is visible. A true solution does not scatter the beam. | Tyndall = beam visible. |
| Brownian movement | Colloidal particles move in a continuous, random zigzag path. | Brownian = zigzag. |

## Colloids (part 2 of 2)

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Centrifuge | A high-speed centrifuge can separate a colloid, which simple filtration cannot. Example: separating cream from milk. | Too small to filter, so spin it. |
| Dispersed phase | The component of a colloid present in the smaller quantity, like a solute. It is the part spread out in the mixture. | Dispersed = the small amount. |
| Dispersion medium | The component of a colloid present in the larger quantity, like a solvent. The particles are spread through it. | Medium = the big amount. |

## Types of colloids (part 1 of 2)

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Aerosol | A solid or a liquid dispersed in a gas. Solid in a gas: smoke and dust. Liquid in a gas: fog, mist, cloud and insecticide sprays. The name aerosol is used for both. | Aerosol = in a gas. |
| Foam | A gas dispersed in a liquid. Examples: whipped cream, froth and soap lather. | Foam = gas bubbles in a liquid. |
| Emulsion | A liquid dispersed in a liquid. Examples: milk, hair cream and butter. | Emulsion = liquid in liquid. |

## Types of colloids (part 2 of 2)

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Sol | A solid dispersed in a liquid. Examples: paints and cell fluids. | Sol = solid in a liquid. |
| Gel | A liquid dispersed in a solid. Examples: cheese and jellies. | Gel = liquid in a solid. |
| Solid sol | A solid or a gas dispersed in a solid. Solid in a solid: colored glass and gemstones. Gas in a solid: pumice stone and foam rubber. The name solid sol is used for both. | Solid sol = in a solid. |
