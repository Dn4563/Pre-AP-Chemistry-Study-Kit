# Deck 1: The Periodic Table

## Metals, nonmetals and metalloids

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Metals | Good conductors of heat and electricity, malleable and ductile. Most elements are metals; they sit to the left of the metalloid staircase and in the inner transition rows at the bottom. | Left of the staircase = metal. |
| Malleable | Can be bent and shaped (hammered into sheets). A property of metals. | Malleable = mallet. |
| Ductile | Can be drawn out into wires. A property of metals. | Ductile = duct wire. |
| Nonmetals | Poor conductors of heat and electricity, and brittle. They sit to the right of the metalloid staircase. Hydrogen is a nonmetal even though it sits on the left. | Right of the staircase = nonmetal (plus H). |
| Metalloids | Also called semimetals: elements with some metallic and some nonmetallic properties. They sit on the staircase (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te on your slide). Aluminum touches the staircase but is a metal. | On the stairs = both. |

## Group names

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Group | A vertical column of the periodic table. Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons, so they have similar properties and bonding tendencies. | Group = column = same valence electrons. |
| Alkali metals | Group 1 (not hydrogen). They form +1 ions. | Group 1, charge +1. |
| Alkaline earth metals | Group 2. They form +2 ions. Two words: "alkaline earth." | Group 2, two words, +2. |
| Transition metals | Groups 3 to 12. Many have variable charges. | The wide middle block. |
| Halogens | Group 17. They form -1 ions. | Halogens grab 1 electron. |
| Noble gases | Group 18 (also called inert gases). Full valence shell, so they tend not to form ions. | Noble = already complete. |

## Families and rows

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Boron, carbon, nitrogen and oxygen families | Group 13 is the boron family, 14 the carbon family, 15 the nitrogen family and 16 the oxygen family. Each is named for its top element. | Named for the element on top. |
| Inner transition metals | The two rows set below the table: the lanthanide series (atomic numbers 58 to 71) and the actinide series (90 to 103). | The two rows at the bottom. |
| Lanthanide series | Inner transition row of atomic numbers 58 to 71, named because it follows lanthanum (La, 57). | Follows La. |
| Actinide series | Inner transition row of atomic numbers 90 to 103, named because it follows actinium (Ac, 89). | Follows Ac. |
| Period | A horizontal row. There are 7 periods. Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells (principal energy levels). | Period = row = shells. |
| States at room temperature | At 25 °C there are 11 gases and 2 liquids on the periodic table; every other element is a solid. | 11 gases, 2 liquids, the rest solid. |

# Deck 2: History of the Atom

## Early ideas

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Leucippus | Early Greek thinker who, with a partner, proposed that matter is made of tiny particles, the idea behind the word atom. | First "tiny particles" idea. |
| Antoine Lavoisier | Came up with the idea that matter cannot be created or destroyed but may change in form (conservation of mass). | Lavoisier: nothing lost. |
| Dalton's atomic theory (1810) | Elements are made of tiny particles called atoms; atoms of a given element are identical; atoms of different elements differ; atoms combine to form compounds with the same relative numbers and types of atoms; atoms are not created or destroyed in chemical reactions, only regrouped. | Dalton = solid-ball atoms that regroup. |
| Law of constant composition | A given compound always has the same relative numbers and types of atoms, bonded in the same ratio. | Same compound, same ratio. |

## Finding the particles

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| J. J. Thomson (1897) | Showed atoms can emit tiny negative particles (electrons), using cathode ray tubes. Reasoned the atom must also hold positive charge to balance them, so atoms have zero overall charge. | Thomson found the electron. |
| Plum pudding model | Thomson's model: a uniform sphere of positive charge with negative electrons scattered inside it. | Electrons are the plums. |
| Ernest Rutherford (1911) | Fired positive alpha particles at thin metal foil. Most went straight through (the atom is mostly empty space); some bounced back (a small, dense, positive center). This disproved the plum pudding model. | Gold foil found the nucleus. |
| Nucleus | The tiny, densely packed, positively charged center of the atom. It holds the protons and neutrons, so nearly all of the atom's mass. | Tiny, dense, positive. |
| James Chadwick (1932) | Bombarded beryllium with alpha particles and found neutral particles with about the same mass as a proton: the neutron. This completed the picture of the nucleus. | Chadwick found the neutron. |

## Where the electrons are

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Niels Bohr (1913) | Proposed that electrons travel around the nucleus in orbits that are fixed energy levels, like planets around the sun. | Bohr = planetary orbits. |
| Energy level | In the Bohr model, a fixed orbit where electrons are found. The period number tells how many principal energy levels an atom has. | Period = number of levels. |
| Problem with Bohr's model | Electrons do not follow exact orbits; we cannot know exactly where an electron is. | Orbits are too exact. |
| Orbitals (modern model) | Regions of space around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found, about 90% of the time. This replaced Bohr's fixed orbits. | Orbital = likely region, not a path. |

# Deck 3: Inside the Atom

## Subatomic particles

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | Positively charged particle in the nucleus. Has mass. Its charge is equal in size to an electron's charge. | Proton = positive. |
| Neutron | Neutral particle in the nucleus with about the same mass as a proton. | Neutron = neutral. |
| Electron | Negatively charged particle found outside the nucleus. Nearly massless. | Electron = negative, outside, light. |
| Number of protons | Sets the element's identity (atomic character). Change the protons and you have a different element. | Protons = name tag. |
| Number of electrons | Determines chemical behavior. Electrons are the parts that bond when atoms combine. | Electrons = chemistry. |

## Counting particles

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic number (Z) | The number of protons. It is the whole number on each periodic table square. | Z = protons. |
| Mass number (A) | The number of protons plus neutrons in one atom. | A = p + n. |
| Neutron count | Neutrons = mass number - atomic number. If no isotope is given, round the atomic mass to get the mass number. | n = A - Z. |
| Electrons in a neutral atom | Equal to the atomic number, because the positive and negative charges must balance. | Neutral: e = p. |
| Electrons in an ion | Electrons = protons - charge. A 2+ ion has 2 fewer electrons than protons; a 3- ion has 3 more. The nucleus does not change. | e = Z - charge. |

## Bohr models and valence electrons

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | Electrons in the outermost energy level. For representative elements the group tells how many (group 1 has 1, group 2 has 2, group 13 has 3, and so on through 18 with 8). | Outer shell = valence. |
| Bohr model drawing | Draw the nucleus, then one ring per principal energy level (the period number), placing the valence electrons (from the group) on the outer ring. | Rings from the period, outer dots from the group. |
| Octet | A complete valence shell of 8 electrons. Only hydrogen and helium have a valence shell that holds 2. | Eight is great. |
| Atomic mass | The decimal number on each square: the average mass of all of an element's isotopes, weighted by how common each one is. | Decimal = average. |

# Deck 4: Isotopes

## What isotopes are

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. | Same protons, different neutrons. |
| Chemistry of isotopes | Isotopes of an element show almost identical chemical properties, because chemistry depends on electrons, not neutrons. | Same electrons, same chemistry. |
| Isotopes in nature | Elements are usually found as a mixture of their isotopes. | Nature mixes them. |
| Hydrogen isotopes | Hydrogen-1 (no neutrons), hydrogen-2 or deuterium (1 neutron) and hydrogen-3 or tritium (2 neutrons). | Protium, deuterium, tritium. |

## Isotopic notation

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Isotopic notation | Written with the mass number (A) as a superscript and the atomic number (Z) as a subscript to the left of the symbol, for example ¹³₆C. An ion's charge goes at the upper right. | Mass on top, protons on the bottom. |
| Hyphen notation | The element name followed by the mass number, for example carbon-13 or Re-187. | Name dash mass. |
| Same element test | Two symbols are the same element only if they have the same atomic number (Z). Same mass number with different Z means different elements. | Check the bottom number. |

## Average atomic mass

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Relative (percent) abundance | How common each isotope of an element is, as a percent or decimal of all its atoms. | How many of each. |
| Weighted average atomic mass | Sum of (each isotope's abundance as a decimal × its mass). This is the decimal atomic mass on the periodic table. | Like a weighted class average. |
| Isotopic mass | The mass of one isotope of an element. In the Candium lab each type of candy played the part of one isotope. | One isotope's mass. |

# Deck 5: Ions and Ionic Compounds

## Forming ions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Ion | An atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons and so carries a charge. The nucleus does not change. | Gained or lost electrons. |
| Cation | A positive ion, formed when an atom loses electrons. Metals tend to form cations. | Cats are paws-itive. |
| Anion | A negative ion, formed when an atom gains electrons. Nonmetals tend to form anions. | A negative ion. |
| Why atoms ionize | To complete their valence shell (an octet). One atom loses electrons and another gains them until both have complete octets. | Everyone wants eight. |
| Metals lose, nonmetals gain | Metals lose electrons to form cations; nonmetals gain electrons to form anions. | Metals give, nonmetals take. |

## Charges by group

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 and group 2 charges | Group 1 forms +1 ions; group 2 forms +2 ions. | Charge = group number. |
| Group 13 charge | Group 13 forms +3 ions (for example Al³⁺ and Ga³⁺). | Lose 3. |
| Groups 15, 16 and 17 charges | Group 15 forms -3, group 16 forms -2, group 17 forms -1 ions. | 8 minus the group's last digit, negative. |
| Noble gas charge | Group 18 tends not to form ions because its valence shell is already complete. | Already done. |
| Transition metal charges | Many transition metals have variable charges (for example Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ or Cu²⁺), so the charge must be given. | Middle block: it varies. |

## Bonds and formulas

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Bond | The sharing or giving and taking of electrons between two atoms. | Share or swap. |
| Ionic bond | The electrostatic force of attraction between a cation and an anion. Forms between a metal and a nonmetal. | Opposites attract. |
| Covalent compound | A compound in which atoms are bonded by sharing electrons, never fully transferring them. Formed between nonmetals. | Co-valent = co-share. |
| Chemical formula | Shows the types of atoms and the number of each in one unit of a compound, using element symbols and subscripts. A subscript of 1 is not written. | Symbols plus subscripts. |
| Ionic formula rule | Write the cation first, then the anion, with enough of each that the charges add to zero. Ionic compounds are electrically neutral. | Charges sum to zero. |
| Crossover method | Swap each ion's charge number to become the other ion's subscript, then reduce to the lowest ratio. Ba²⁺ and P³⁻ give Ba₃P₂. | Cross the charges. |
| Properties of ionic compounds | Strong and brittle, with high melting points. They conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water, not as solids. | Hard, high-melting, conduct when free to move. |

## Common polyatomic ions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Polyatomic ion | A charged group of two or more atoms covalently bonded together. It stays together as one unit in a formula; use parentheses if you need more than one, as in Ca(CN)₂. | One ion, many atoms. |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺, the common positive polyatomic ion. | The one plus. |
| Hydroxide and peroxide | Hydroxide is OH⁻; peroxide is O₂²⁻. | OH one minus. |
| Cyanide and acetate | Cyanide is CN⁻; acetate is C₂H₃O₂⁻. | Both -1. |
| Nitrate and nitrite | Nitrate is NO₃⁻; nitrite is NO₂⁻. | -ate has one more O than -ite. |
| Carbonate and hydrogen carbonate | Carbonate is CO₃²⁻; hydrogen carbonate (bicarbonate) is HCO₃⁻. | Add H, charge goes up by 1. |
| Sulfate, sulfite and hydrogen sulfate | Sulfate is SO₄²⁻, sulfite is SO₃²⁻, hydrogen sulfate is HSO₄⁻. | -ate 4 O, -ite 3 O. |
| Thiosulfate | S₂O₃²⁻. | Thio = an extra S. |
| Phosphate | PO₄³⁻. | Phosphate, -3. |
| Chlorine oxyanions | Hypochlorite ClO⁻, chlorite ClO₂⁻, chlorate ClO₃⁻, perchlorate ClO₄⁻. All -1. | Hypo 1, ite 2, ate 3, per 4. |
| Chromate and dichromate | Chromate is CrO₄²⁻; dichromate is Cr₂O₇²⁻. | Both -2. |
| Permanganate | MnO₄⁻. | Purple ion, -1. |

# Deck 6: Molar Mass and the Mole

## Molar mass

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Molar mass | The mass of one mole of a substance in grams per mole (g/mol). Add up the atomic masses of every atom in the formula. | Add them all up. |
| Molar mass with subscripts | Multiply each element's atomic mass by its subscript before adding. MgCl₂ = 24.31 + 2(35.45) = 95.21 g/mol. | Count every atom. |
| Molar mass with parentheses | A subscript after parentheses multiplies everything inside. Ca(CN)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 C and 2 N. | The outside number multiplies the inside. |

## Mole conversions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Mole | The counting unit for particles: one mole holds 6.022 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules or formula units). | A chemist's dozen. |
| Avogadro's number | 6.022 × 10²³, the number of particles in one mole. | 6.022 × 10²³ per mole. |
| Grams to moles | Divide by the molar mass: grams × (1 mol / molar mass g). | Grams ÷ molar mass. |
| Subscript ratio | The subscripts give the mole ratio between a compound and its atoms: 1 mol CCl₄ contains 4 mol Cl. | The subscript is the ratio. |
| Roadmap to atoms | grams → (÷ molar mass) → moles of compound → (× subscript) → moles of atoms → (× 6.022 × 10²³) → number of atoms. | Grams, moles, moles, atoms. |
| Formula unit | One unit of an ionic compound, such as one Al₂S₃. Ionic compounds are counted in formula units, not molecules. | Ionic = formula units. |
