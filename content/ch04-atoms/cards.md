# Deck 1: The Periodic Table

## Metals, nonmetals and metalloids

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Metals | Good conductors of heat and electricity that can be bent into shape and drawn into wires. Most elements are this type; they sit left of the staircase and in the two bottom rows. | Left of the staircase = metal. |
| Malleable | Can be bent and shaped (hammered into sheets). A property of metals. | Malleable = mallet. |
| Ductile | Can be drawn out into wires. A property of metals. | Ductile = duct wire. |
| Nonmetals | Poor conductors of heat and electricity that are brittle. They sit right of the staircase, plus hydrogen, even though hydrogen sits on the left. | Right of the staircase = nonmetal (plus H). |
| Metalloids | Also called semimetals: elements with some metallic and some nonmetallic properties. They sit on the staircase (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te on your slide). Aluminum touches the staircase but is a metal. | On the stairs = both. |

## Group names

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Group | A vertical column of the periodic table. Elements in one column share the same number of valence electrons, so they have similar properties and bonding tendencies. | Group = column = same valence electrons. |
| Alkali metals | Group 1 (not hydrogen). They form +1 ions. | Group 1, charge +1. |
| Alkaline earth metals | The elements in column 2, such as Mg, Ca and Sr. They form +2 ions. | Group 2, two words, +2. |
| Transition metals | Groups 3 to 12. Many have variable charges. | The wide middle block. |
| Halogens | Group 17. They form -1 ions. | Halogens grab 1 electron. |
| Noble gases | Column 18, also called the inert elements. Their valence shell is already full, so they tend not to form ions. | Noble = already complete. |

## Families and rows

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Boron family | Column 13, named for the element at its top (B). These elements form +3 ions. | 13 = boron. |
| Carbon family | Column 14, named for the element at its top (C). It has no single ion charge to memorize (4+ or 4-). | 14 = carbon. |
| Nitrogen family | Column 15, named for the element at its top (N). These elements form -3 ions. | 15 = nitrogen. |
| Oxygen family | Column 16, named for the element at its top (O). These elements form -2 ions. | 16 = oxygen. |
| Inner transition metals | The two rows set below the table: the lanthanide series (atomic numbers 58 to 71) and the actinide series (90 to 103). | The two rows at the bottom. |
| Lanthanide series | Inner transition row of atomic numbers 58 to 71, named because it follows lanthanum (La, 57). | Follows La. |
| Actinide series | The inner transition row of atomic numbers 90 to 103, named for element 89 (Ac), which comes just before it. | Follows Ac. |
| Period | A horizontal row of the table of elements; there are 7. Elements in one row have the same number of electron shells (principal energy levels). | Period = row = shells. |
| States at room temperature | At 25 °C there are 11 gases and 2 liquids on the periodic table; every other element is a solid. | 11 gases, 2 liquids, the rest solid. |

# Deck 2: History of the Atom

## Early ideas

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Leucippus | Early Greek thinker who, with a partner, proposed that matter is made of tiny particles, the idea behind the word atom. | First "tiny particles" idea. |
| Antoine Lavoisier | Came up with the idea that matter cannot be created or destroyed but may change in form (conservation of mass). | Lavoisier: nothing lost. |
| Dalton's atomic theory (1810) | Elements are made of tiny particles called atoms; atoms of a given element are identical; atoms of different elements differ; atoms combine to form compounds with the same relative numbers and types of atoms; atoms are not created or destroyed in chemical reactions, only regrouped. | Dalton = solid-ball atoms that regroup. |
| Law of constant composition | A given compound always has the same relative numbers and types of atoms, bonded in the same ratio. | Same compound, same ratio. |

## Finding the particles

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| J. J. Thomson (1897) | Showed atoms can emit tiny negative particles (electrons), using cathode ray tubes. Reasoned the atom must also hold positive charge to balance them, so atoms have zero overall charge. | Thomson found the electron. |
| Plum pudding model | Thomson's picture of the atom: a uniform sphere of positive charge with negative electrons scattered inside it. | Electrons are the plums. |
| Ernest Rutherford (1911) | Fired positive alpha particles at thin metal foil. Most went straight through (the atom is mostly empty space); some bounced back (a small, dense, positive center). This disproved the plum pudding model. | Gold foil found the nucleus. |
| Nucleus | The tiny, densely packed, positively charged center of the atom. It holds the protons and neutrons, so nearly all of the atom's mass. | Tiny, dense, positive. |
| James Chadwick (1932) | Bombarded beryllium with alpha particles and found neutral particles with about the same mass as a proton: the neutron. This completed the picture of the nucleus. | Chadwick found the neutron. |

## Where the electrons are

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Niels Bohr (1913) | Proposed that electrons travel around the nucleus in orbits that are fixed energy levels, like planets around the sun. | Bohr = planetary orbits. |
| Energy level | In the Bohr model, a fixed orbit where electrons are found. The row (period) number tells how many of these an atom has. | Period = number of levels. |
| Problem with Bohr's model | Electrons do not follow exact orbits; we cannot know exactly where an electron is. | Orbits are too exact. |
| Orbitals (modern model) | Regions of space around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found, about 90% of the time. This replaced Bohr's fixed orbits. | Orbital = likely region, not a path. |

# Deck 3: Inside the Atom

## Subatomic particles

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | Positively charged particle in the nucleus. Has mass. Its charge is equal in size to an electron's charge. | Proton = positive. |
| Neutron | Neutral particle in the nucleus with about the same mass as a proton. | Neutron = neutral. |
| Electron | Negatively charged particle found outside the nucleus. Nearly massless. | Electron = negative, outside, light. |
| Number of protons | Sets which element an atom is (its atomic character). Change this count and you have a different element. | Protons = name tag. |
| Number of electrons | Determines chemical behavior, because these particles are the ones that bond when atoms combine. | Electrons = chemistry. |

## Counting particles

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic number (Z) | How many protons an atom has: the whole value shown on each periodic table square. | Z = protons. |
| Mass number (A) | Protons plus neutrons in one atom. | A = p + n. |
| Neutron count | Mass number minus atomic number. If no isotope is given, round the atomic mass to get the mass number. | n = A - Z. |
| Electrons in a neutral atom | Equal to the proton count (Z), because the positive and negative charges must balance. | Neutral: e = p. |
| Electrons in an ion | Protons minus the charge. A 2+ ion has 2 fewer negative particles than protons; a 3- ion has 3 more. The nucleus does not change. | e = Z - charge. |

## Bohr models and valence electrons

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Valence electrons | The negative particles in the outermost energy level. For representative elements the group tells how many (group 1 has 1, group 2 has 2, group 13 has 3, and so on through 18 with 8). | Outer shell = valence. |
| Bohr model drawing | Draw the nucleus, then one ring per principal energy level (the period number), placing the valence electrons (from the group) on the outer ring. | Rings from the period, outer dots from the group. |
| Octet | A complete valence shell of 8 electrons. Only hydrogen and helium have a valence shell that holds 2. | Eight is great. |
| Atomic mass | The decimal value on each periodic table square: the average of all of an element's isotopes, weighted by how common each one is. | Decimal = average. |

# Deck 4: Isotopes

## What isotopes are

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Isotopes | Atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. | Same protons, different neutrons. |
| Chemistry of isotopes | Atoms of one element with different neutron counts react almost identically, because how an atom reacts depends on its electrons, not its neutrons. | Same electrons, same chemistry. |
| Isotopes in nature | Elements are usually found as a mixture of atoms with different neutron counts. | Nature mixes them. |
| Hydrogen-1 | The most common form of H: 1 proton and no neutrons, mass number 1. | Plain hydrogen, no neutron. |
| Deuterium | Hydrogen-2: 1 proton and 1 neutron, mass number 2. | Deu = two. |
| Tritium | Hydrogen-3: 1 proton and 2 neutrons, mass number 3. | Tri = three. |

## Isotopic notation

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Isotopic notation | Written with the mass number (A) as a superscript and the atomic number (Z) as a subscript to the left of the symbol, for example ¹³₆C. An ion's charge goes at the upper right. | Mass on top, protons on the bottom. |
| Hyphen notation | The element name followed by the mass number, for example carbon-13 or Re-187. | Name dash mass. |
| Same element test | Two symbols match only if they share an atomic number (Z). Equal mass numbers with different Z values are different substances. | Check the bottom number. |

## Average atomic mass

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Relative (percent) abundance | How common each isotope of an element is, as a share or decimal of all its atoms. | How many of each. |
| Weighted average atomic mass | The sum of (each isotope's abundance as a decimal × how heavy that isotope is). This is the decimal value shown on the periodic table. | Like a weighted class average. |
| Isotopic mass | How heavy one particular form of an element is. In the Candium lab each type of candy played the part of one form. | One isotope's mass. |

# Deck 5: Ions and Ionic Compounds

## Forming ions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Ion | An atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons and so carries a charge. The nucleus does not change. | Gained or lost electrons. |
| Cation | A positive ion, formed when an atom loses electrons. Metals tend to form this kind of ion. | Cats are paws-itive. |
| Anion | A negative ion, formed when an atom gains electrons. Nonmetals tend to form this kind of ion. | A negative ion. |
| Why atoms ionize | To complete their valence shell (an octet). One particle gives up electrons and another takes them until both have complete octets. | Everyone wants eight. |
| Metals lose, nonmetals gain | Elements left of the staircase give up electrons and become cations; elements right of it take electrons and become anions. | Metals give, nonmetals take. |

## Charges by group

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Group 1 charge | Alkali metals lose 1 valence electron and form +1 ions, such as Na⁺ and K⁺. | One electron out, +1. |
| Group 2 charge | Alkaline earth metals lose 2 valence electrons and form +2 ions, such as Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺. | Two electrons out, +2. |
| Group 13 charge | Column 13 forms +3 ions (for example Al³⁺ and Ga³⁺). | Lose 3. |
| Group 15 charge | Nitrogen family nonmetals gain 3 electrons to complete the octet and form -3 ions, such as N³⁻ and P³⁻. | 5 + 3 = 8. |
| Group 16 charge | Oxygen family nonmetals gain 2 electrons and form -2 ions, such as O²⁻ and S²⁻. | 6 + 2 = 8. |
| Group 17 charge | Halogens gain 1 electron and form -1 ions, such as F⁻ and Cl⁻. | 7 + 1 = 8. |
| Noble gas charge | Group 18 tends not to form ions because its valence shell is already complete. | Already done. |
| Transition metal charges | Many elements in columns 3 to 12 can form ions of different sizes (for example Fe²⁺ or Fe³⁺, Cu⁺ or Cu²⁺), so a problem must tell you which one. | Middle block: it varies. |

## Bonds and formulas

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Bond | The sharing or giving and taking of electrons between two atoms. | Share or swap. |
| Ionic bond | The electrostatic force of attraction between a cation and an anion. Forms between a metal and a nonmetal. | Opposites attract. |
| Covalent compound | A substance in which atoms are held together by sharing electrons, never fully transferring them. Formed between nonmetals. | Co-valent = co-share. |
| Chemical formula | Shows the types of atoms and the number of each in one unit of a compound, using element symbols and subscripts. A subscript of 1 is not written. | Symbols plus subscripts. |
| Ionic formula rule | Write the cation first, then the anion, with enough of each that the charges add to zero. The finished compound is electrically neutral. | Charges sum to zero. |
| Crossover method | Swap each ion's charge number to become the other ion's subscript, then reduce to the lowest ratio. Ba²⁺ and P³⁻ give Ba₃P₂. | Cross the charges. |
| Polyatomic ion | A charged group of two or more atoms covalently bonded together. It stays together as one unit in a formula; use parentheses if you need more than one, as in Ca(CN)₂. | One ion, many atoms. |
| Properties of ionic compounds | Strong and brittle, with high melting points. They conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water, not as solids. | Hard, high-melting, conduct when free to move. |

# Deck 6: Molar Mass and the Mole

## Molar mass

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Molar mass | The grams in one mole of a substance (g/mol). Add up the atomic weights of every atom in the formula. | Add them all up. |
| Molar mass with subscripts | Multiply each element's atomic weight by the small number after its symbol before adding. MgCl₂ = 24.31 + 2(35.45) = 95.21 g/mol. | Count every atom. |
| Molar mass with parentheses | A subscript after a bracketed group multiplies everything inside it. Ca(CN)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 C and 2 N. | The outside number multiplies the inside. |

## Mole conversions

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Mole | The chemist's counting unit: 6.022 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules or formula units). | A chemist's dozen. |
| Avogadro's number | 6.022 × 10²³, the count of particles in one mole. | 6.022 × 10²³ per mole. |
| Grams to moles | Divide a sample's mass by its molar mass: mass × (1 mol / molar mass). | Grams ÷ molar mass. |
| Subscript ratio | The small numbers in a formula tell how many moles of each element are in one mole of compound: 1 mol CCl₄ contains 4 mol Cl. | The subscript is the ratio. |
| Roadmap to atoms | grams → (÷ molar mass) → moles of compound → (× subscript) → moles of one element → (× 6.022 × 10²³) → particle count. | Grams, moles, moles, atoms. |
| Formula unit | The smallest repeating piece of an ionic compound, such as one Al₂S₃. Ionic compounds are counted this way, not in molecules. | Ionic = formula units. |
