# Deck 1: Formulas and Bonds (5.1)

> Chapter 5 is about naming compounds and writing their formulas. Before naming anything, you need to know what kind of compound you have: ionic (made of ions) or covalent (made of nonmetals sharing electrons), and whether the formula is simplified.

## Kinds of formulas

> A formula can be empirical (reduced to the simplest whole-number ratio) or molecular (every atom actually present). Ionic compounds are always written in the simplest ratio. Covalent compounds can be written either way, so you never reduce a covalent formula.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Empirical formula | Atom counts reduced to the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. | Simplified. |
| Molecular formula | Shows every atom actually present in one unit of the substance; it may or may not reduce. | All atoms present. |
| Ionic formulas are simplified | Compounds made of ions must always be written in the lowest ratio, such as CaO, never Ca₂O₂. | Ionic = always reduce. |
| Covalent formulas are not reduced | Compounds of nonmetals keep the exact atom counts, so N₂O₄ stays N₂O₄. | Covalent = keep as is. |

## Bond types

> Ionic bonds form between a metal and a nonmetal after ions form: a cation and an anion pull on each other. Covalent bonds form between two nonmetals that share electrons, with no ions formed. Ionic bonds are stronger because of the electrostatic attraction between opposite charges. Compounds made of exactly two elements are binary.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic bond | The attraction between a cation and an anion, usually a metal with a nonmetal. | Metal + nonmetal. |
| Covalent bond | Two nonmetals sharing electrons; no ions form. | Nonmetal + nonmetal. |
| Electrostatic force of attraction | The pull between opposite charges that makes ionic bonds stronger than covalent bonds. | Opposites attract. |
| Binary compound | A substance made of exactly two elements; metal + nonmetal pairs and two-nonmetal pairs (Types I, II and III) are this kind. | Bi = two. |
| The five types | I: representative metal + nonmetal; II: transition metal + nonmetal; III: two nonmetals; IV: contains a polyatomic ion; V: acids. | Five naming systems. |

# Deck 2: Main-Group Metal + Nonmetal (Type I, 5.2)

> Type I compounds pair a representative (main-group) metal, which forms only one ion, with a nonmetal. Name the ions; ignore the subscripts.

## Naming main-group metal compounds

> The cation comes first and keeps the element's name. The anion comes second: take the root of the element's name and add -ide. Ionic compounds are named for their ions, so subscripts never show up in the name: Na₂O is sodium oxide, not disodium oxide.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Type I: main-group metal + nonmetal | An ionic compound whose cation has only one possible charge, such as Na⁺ or Mg²⁺; no numeral, no prefixes. | One-charge metal. |
| Cation first | In every ionic name and formula, the positive ion comes before the negative one. | Metal first. |
| Simple cation name | A main-group metal ion keeps the element's label unchanged: Na⁺ is sodium. | Name unchanged. |
| -ide ending | A one-atom anion is named with the root of the element plus this suffix: Cl⁻ is chloride, O²⁻ is oxide. | Chlorine → chloride. |
| Subscripts stay out of ionic names | Compounds of ions are called by their ions only, so Mg₃N₂ is magnesium nitride, not trimagnesium dinitride. | No di-, no tri-. |
| Anion charge = group − 8 | For a nonmetal, subtract 8 from its 1A–8A column number: sulfur in 6A gives 6 − 8 = −2, so S²⁻. | 6A → 2−, 7A → 1−. |

## Writing their formulas

> Find each ion's charge from its group (group 1 = 1+, group 2 = 2+, group 13 = 3+, group 15 = 3−, group 16 = 2−, group 17 = 1−). If the charges already add to zero, just write cation then anion. If not, crisscross: each ion's charge number becomes the other ion's subscript, then reduce.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Neutral compound | The total positive charge must equal the total negative charge, so the charges add to zero. | Charges sum to 0. |
| Crisscross method | Each ion's charge number becomes the other ion's subscript: Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give Al₂O₃. | Criss-cross applesauce. |
| Reduce after crossing | If the crossed subscripts share a factor, divide it out: Ca²⁺ and O²⁻ give CaO, not Ca₂O₂. | Simplest ratio. |

# Deck 3: Variable-Charge Metal + Nonmetal (Type II, 5.2)

> Type II compounds contain a metal that can form more than one charge, usually a transition metal. The name has to say which charge, using a Roman numeral.

## Roman numerals

> The cation is still named first and the anion still ends in -ide. The difference: in parentheses after the metal, a Roman numeral gives the metal's charge. When naming from a formula, work backward from the anion's total charge to find the metal's charge.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Type II: variable metal + nonmetal | An ionic compound whose cation can carry more than one charge, so the name gives it as a Roman numeral, such as iron(II) or iron(III). | Metal with options. |
| Roman numeral | The metal's charge, written in parentheses right after its name: iron(III) is Fe³⁺. | Shows the charge. |
| Working out the numeral | Total negative charge ÷ number of metal atoms = metal's charge: Fe₂O₃ has 3 × 2− = 6−, so each Fe is 3+. | Work backward. |
| Numeral ≠ subscript | The Roman number in a name never tells how many atoms there are: CuCl₂ is copper(II) chloride because Cu is 2+. | Charge, not subscript. |

## The exceptions

> Not every transition metal needs a numeral, and not every numeral metal is a transition metal. Zinc, silver and cadmium form only one ion each, so they never get a numeral (your OWL assignment adds aluminum, Al³⁺, to that list). Lead and tin are representative metals that can form two ions, so they always need one.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc | Always Zn²⁺, so its compounds never get a Roman numeral, as in ZnCl₂. | No numeral. |
| Silver | Always Ag⁺, so its compounds never get a Roman numeral, as in AgBr. | No numeral. |
| Cadmium | Always Cd²⁺, so its compounds never get a Roman numeral, as in CdF₂. | No numeral. |
| Lead | A representative metal with two possible charges (2+ or 4+), so it needs a numeral, as in PbS₂ (the 4+ form). | Needs a numeral. |
| Tin | A representative metal with two possible charges (2+ or 4+), so it needs a numeral: SnCl₄ is tin(IV) chloride. | Needs a numeral. |
| Aluminum (one charge) | Always Al³⁺, so its compounds never get a Roman numeral, as in AlCl₃. | Fourth no-numeral metal. |

# Deck 4: Two Nonmetals, Covalent (Type III, 5.3)

> Type III compounds are made of two nonmetals. Because nonmetals can combine in many ratios, the name uses prefixes to say exactly how many atoms of each element there are.

## Naming rules

> Name the first element in full. Name the second like an anion, with -ide. Put a prefix on each to show the number of atoms, except that mono- is never used on the first element. When the element name starts with a or o, drop the prefix's last vowel: monoxide, pentoxide, not monooxide.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Type III: two nonmetals | A covalent compound, named with prefixes that count each kind of atom. | Prefixes. |
| Naming the first nonmetal | It is written out completely, unchanged: carbon, sulfur, nitrogen. | Full name first. |
| Naming the second nonmetal | It takes an anion-style ending: oxygen becomes oxide, chlorine becomes chloride. | Ends in -ide. |
| No mono- on the first element | If the formula starts with just one atom of something, that atom gets no prefix: CO₂ is carbon dioxide. | Never "monocarbon." |
| Drop the vowel before a or o | When the element starts with a or o, the prefix loses its last a or o: CO is carbon monoxide, N₂O₅ is dinitrogen pentoxide. | Mono-oxide → monoxide. |
| Prefixes only for covalent | Number words like di- and tri- are used for compounds of nonmetals (and for water in hydrates), never for ions: Na₂O is not "disodium oxide." | Ionic = no prefixes. |
| Water | The everyday name used for H₂O instead of dihydrogen monoxide. | H₂O. |
| Ammonia | The everyday name used for NH₃ instead of nitrogen trihydride. | NH₃. |

## The prefixes

> Ten prefixes, one for each number from 1 to 10. Learn them in order; the test review asks you to list them.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| mono- | 1 | Monorail. |
| di- | 2 | Dialogue: two voices. |
| tri- | 3 | Triangle. |
| tetra- | 4 | Tetris pieces have 4 blocks. |
| penta- | 5 | Pentagon. |
| hexa- | 6 | Hexagon. |
| hepta- | 7 | Heptathlon: 7 events. |
| octa- | 8 | Octopus. |
| nona- | 9 | Nonagon. |
| deca- | 10 | Decade. |

## Writing covalent formulas

> Writing is the reverse: write the two symbols in order, then turn each prefix into a subscript. No crisscrossing and no reducing; the prefixes already give the exact numbers.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Prefixes become subscripts | Each number word gives the small number written after the symbol: dinitrogen tetroxide is N₂O₄. | Read the numbers. |
| No crisscross for covalent | Nonmetal-only compounds don't form ions, so charges are never crossed: sulfur trioxide is SO₃. | No charges to cross. |
| Diatomic elements | H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂: substances too reactive to exist as single atoms. | Seven come in pairs. |

# Deck 5: Polyatomic Ions (Type IV, 5.5)

> Type IV compounds contain a polyatomic ion: a group of atoms with one overall charge. You'll have an ion sheet on the test, so the skill is using it: find the ion, use its charge, and never change it.

## Polyatomic ions

> A polyatomic ion is a group of covalently bonded atoms with an overall charge. Think of it like a kid's meal: the charge and the atoms inside can't change. Oxyanions are polyatomic ions with the same element and different numbers of oxygens, such as sulfate (SO₄²⁻) and sulfite (SO₃²⁻).

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Polyatomic ion | A group of covalently bonded atoms that carries one overall charge. | One ion, many atoms. |
| Never change the ion | Its atoms, subscripts and charge are fixed; only the number of units you use can vary. | Kid's meal: no swaps. |
| Oxyanion | A polyatomic anion of one element with oxygen; different numbers of oxygen give different names. | Sulfate vs. sulfite. |
| -ate vs. -ite | The -ite ion has one fewer oxygen than the -ate ion with the same charge: nitrate NO₃⁻, nitrite NO₂⁻. | -ite = one less O. |
| Extra prefixes on oxyanions | per-…-ate has one more oxygen than -ate (perchlorate ClO₄⁻); hypo-…-ite has one fewer than -ite (hypochlorite ClO⁻). | Per = more, hypo = less. |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺, the common polyatomic cation; it goes first like a metal, as in NH₄Cl. | The positive one. |
| Cyanide and hydroxide | The two common polyatomic ions with an -ide ending (CN⁻ and OH⁻), even though -ide usually means a one-atom anion. | -ide exceptions. |

## Naming and writing polyatomic compounds

> Name the cation first (with a Roman numeral if it's a transition metal), then the polyatomic ion's name exactly as it appears on your sheet. To write the formula, balance the charges like before; if you need more than one polyatomic ion, put it in parentheses with the subscript outside.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Type IV: contains a polyatomic ion | A compound built around a charged group of atoms such as NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻ or NH₄⁺. | More than two elements. |
| Polyatomic name as given | The ion's label is copied unchanged from the sheet: Mg(NO₃)₂ is magnesium nitrate. | Copy it from the sheet. |
| Parentheses for more than one | Wrap the ion in brackets with the subscript outside when you need two or more: Ca(OH)₂. | Ca(OH)₂, not CaOH₂. |
| No parentheses for one | With only one polyatomic ion, skip the brackets: NaNO₃, not Na(NO₃). | One unit, no brackets. |
| Variable metals with polyatomic ions | A compound with a charged group like phosphate still shows iron's or copper's charge in Roman numerals: Fe₃(PO₄)₂ is iron(II) phosphate. | Check the metal. |

# Deck 6: Acids (Type V, 5.6)

> Acids are compounds that release H⁺ ions in water. You can spot them because H comes first in the formula. How you name one depends on whether the anion contains oxygen.

## Acids without oxygen

> If the anion has no oxygen, the acid gets the prefix hydro- and the ending -ic on the root of the element: HCl is hydrochloric acid. Cyanide (CN⁻) has no oxygen either, so HCN is hydrocyanic acid.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Type V: acid | A compound that produces H⁺ ions when dissolved in water; its formula starts with H. | H first. |
| Binary acid | Hydrogen with a one-element anion (or cyanide); named hydro- + root + -ic: HBr is hydrobromic. | Hydro…ic. |
| (aq) | The label showing a compound is dissolved in water; written after binary acid formulas such as HCl(aq). | Aqueous. |
| Hydrogen chloride vs. hydrochloric acid | The same formula, HCl, gets the first label as a pure compound and the second when dissolved in water, HCl(aq). | Pure vs. dissolved. |

## Acids with oxygen

> If the anion is a polyatomic ion with oxygen, use the ion's name and change the ending. -ate becomes -ic; -ite becomes -ous. per- and hypo- stay on. No hydro- prefix.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| -ate → -ic | An acid made from an -ate ion ends in -ic: chlorate gives chloric acid, sulfate gives sulfuric acid. | I ATE something ICky. |
| -ite → -ous | An acid made from an -ite ion ends in -ous: chlorite gives chlorous acid, sulfite gives sulfurous acid. | -ite → -ous. |
| per-ate → per-ic | A per-…-ate ion gives a per-…-ic acid: perchlorate gives perchloric acid. | Keep the per-. |
| hypo-ite → hypo-ous | Keeping the prefix, the -ite ending becomes -ous: hypochlorite (ClO⁻) gives hypochlorous acid. | Keep the hypo-. |
| No hydro- for oxyacids | Acids made from oxygen-containing ions never get that prefix: H₂SO₄ is sulfuric acid, not hydrosulfuric. | Hydro only without O. |

# Deck 7: Hydrates and Putting It Together

> The last part of the chapter: hydrates (compounds with water attached), the step-by-step key for deciding which type you have, and the mistakes the review asks you to fix.

## Hydrates

> A hydrate has water molecules built into its crystal, written after a dot: MgSO₄·7H₂O. Name the compound by its usual rules, then add a prefix for the number of waters and the word hydrate.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrate | A compound with water molecules attached, written with a dot: CuSO₄·5H₂O. | Water attached. |
| Naming hydrates | Compound name, then a prefix for the number of waters, as in MgSO₄·7H₂O: magnesium sulfate heptahydrate. | Prefix + hydrate. |

## Which type is it?

> Your dichotomous key asks the same questions every time. Does it start with H? It's an acid. Otherwise, does it have only two elements? If yes and there's a metal, it's Type I or II (check whether the metal needs a numeral). If yes and it's two nonmetals, it's Type III. If it has more than two elements, it has a polyatomic ion: Type IV.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Starts with H | First question in the key: if the formula begins with hydrogen, it's an acid (Type V). | H → acid. |
| Two elements with a metal | A binary compound containing Na, Fe, Cu or Ca is made of ions: Type I if its cation has only one possible charge, Type II if it needs a numeral. | Metal → ionic. |
| Two nonmetals | A binary compound with no metal is covalent: Type III, use prefixes. | No metal → prefixes. |
| More than two elements | If a non-acid has three or more kinds of atoms, it contains a polyatomic ion: Type IV. | Three+ → polyatomic. |

## Common mistakes

> The review packet asks you to find and fix wrong names and formulas. These are the mistakes it tests.

| Term | Definition | Hook |
|---|---|---|
| Missing Roman numeral | A variable metal without its charge, such as "iron nitrate" or "tin hypochlorite." | Which iron? |
| Extra Roman numeral | A charge label on a one-charge metal, such as "zinc(II) hydroxide" (should be zinc hydroxide). | Zn, Ag, Cd: none. |
| Prefixes on ionic names | Using di-, tri- for ions, such as "dipotassium chromate" (should be potassium chromate). | Prefixes are covalent only. |
| Numeral in the formula | Writing the charge inside the symbols, such as Fe(III)Cl₃ (should be FeCl₃). | Numerals go in names only. |
| Anion first | Writing the negative ion before the cation, such as SO₄Cu (should be CuSO₄) or "sulfate aluminum." | Cation first. |
| Missing parentheses | Writing CaOH₂ when you need two hydroxide ions (should be Ca(OH)₂). | Wrap the polyatomic. |
